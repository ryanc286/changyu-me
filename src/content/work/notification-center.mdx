The Activity tab was one of the most used screens at Carousell. 80% of daily active users opened it to check on their account and orders. But important order updates were buried under marketing banners and promotions, and people did not know what to do next. I redesigned it to help people act on what matters and finish more orders.

## The audit

I looked at six months of data on each section's share of taps and reviewed the information architecture. Three problems stood out:

- **Marketing took the space, not the taps.** Banners, company news and promotions got few taps but filled much of the screen.
- **No clear hierarchy.** Every section looked equally important, so nothing guided the eye.
- **Important updates were hard to find.** The account and listing updates people actually used were pushed down the page.

## Learning from other products

I looked at how other products handle notifications. Three patterns kept coming up: **categories**, a **visual hierarchy**, and **read or unread markers**.

## A turning point

I turned those patterns into concepts, but the concepts kept multiplying, and team discussions went back and forth without a clear direction. Competitors showed what other products do, not what our users need. So I stopped making concepts and proposed interviews instead.

<Media id="early-concepts" label="Early concepts that kept multiplying" src="/images/notification-center/early-concepts.webp" />

## User interviews

I talked to 5 people across our four main groups: buyers, sellers, people who do both, and pro sellers. They sorted example notifications into groups while thinking out loud, then tried three concepts.

<Media id="research" label="Card sorting with five users" />

People group notifications in two ways: by topic (buying, selling, promotions) and by urgency. Concept B matched that best and felt the smoothest to use.

<Media id="concepts" label="Concepts A, B and C" />

<Hypothesis>If notifications follow how people already group them, by topic and urgency, people will spot and act on the important ones faster.</Hypothesis>

## More details

<Media id="design" label="Urgent items first, grouped by order" />

- **Urgent items on top.** Notifications are grouped by topic and urgency. Urgent ones get a countdown and a clear button.
- **One timeline per order.** Updates from the same order stack into one item that opens up, so nothing gets lost and the list stays short.
- **Small things bundled.** Low-stakes updates of the same type, like item likes, are combined into one.
- **Marketing as notifications.** Banners became notification-style items. They are easier to scan, and campaigns still get seen.

## Bringing marketing along

Changing marketing placements on a high-traffic page was the riskiest part. Those spots belonged to the marketing team. Instead of trading opinions, I showed six months of tap data on the old banners. To make sure the new format wasn't a downgrade, the notification-style placement takes images, text or video, so campaigns keep their range. Marketing agreed, and a joint workshop settled how campaign notifications would work.

## Responsive web design

The web had no Activity tab before. I designed a responsive version, so people see the same updates on any screen.

The change touched many teams, so I presented the design at the tech town hall, to the whole tech org including C-level leaders, and took their questions.

## Reflection

Evidence before exploration. I spent too long on concepts from competitor research before understanding our own users. Competitors give ideas, not proof. Five interviews settled weeks of debate in a few days.

One screen also serves many people. A layout that helps a casual buyer can slow down a seller with dozens of orders. Naming those tensions early, and checking past research, kept us from rebuilding ideas that had already been tried.
