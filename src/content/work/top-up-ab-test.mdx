Many PayPay users top up small amounts, around ¥5,000, several times a month. Every top-up costs PayPay a processing fee, and across millions of top-ups a month, these small and frequent ones added up fast. Fewer, larger top-ups would save money for the business and save users from doing the same task again and again. I set out to help people top up more at once, without pushing them.

## What I found

I looked at interviews and usage data and found three reasons people top up small amounts:

- **They don't feel safe keeping a big balance.** Users told us they worry about holding too much money in the app.
- **Typing the amount is a hassle.** Usage data showed people dropping off at the amount input step.
- **They don't know how much they spend.** Most users have no idea what they spend on PayPay in a month, so they top up just enough for now.

## Picking what to test first

The first two reasons needed big changes. Making people feel safe with a larger balance meant policy work with the risk and compliance teams, and a new amount input would take months to build. The team's goal this quarter was quick A/B tests that could show results fast, so we started with the third reason: people don't know how much they spent last month. It was quick to ship and easy to measure.

<Hypothesis>If users see how much they spent last month while topping up, they will have a clear reference and top up a larger amount.</Hypothesis>

## Design

The goal was simple: show people what they spent last month, and make it easy to top up that amount.

I explored three ways to do it: an info banner, an extra quick-amount button and a floating button.

<Media id="concepts" label="Three ways to show last month's spending: an info banner, a quick-amount button and a floating button" src="/images/top-up-ab-test/concepts.webp" />

Working through them, I realized the real question was not which layout looked best. It was whether the suggestion works better as a message on top of the flow, or as part of the flow itself. The floating button could cover content at the bottom of the screen, so I dropped it and tested the two ends:

- **Variant A, on top of the flow.** An info banner above the amount buttons, with the recommended amount and a link to top up.
- **Variant B, part of the flow.** The amount as the first quick-amount button, marked おすすめ (Recommended), built from components that already existed.

Both filled in the amount with one tap, so the only real difference was where the suggestion lived.

<Media id="variants" label="Variant A (banner) and Variant B (quick-amount button)" />

## Details that mattered

- **No auto-fill.** My first version filled in the amount for users. It felt too pushy, so the suggestion became something users choose to tap.
- **Right timing.** Users top up about every two weeks, so the suggestion shows at the start of the month, then hides for two weeks.
- **Clean numbers.** One-time spikes like investment payments are left out, and the amount is rounded up to the nearest ¥1,000. ¥14,563 becomes ¥15,000.

## Results

Each variant went to 1% of users for one month, with everyone else as the control group. Variant B, the quick-amount button, was the clear winner. The banner made no significant difference.

## Before rollout

A bigger balance means more risk if an account is taken over. Before the full rollout, I worked with the risk and compliance teams to check balance limits and fraud monitoring. Security tickets did not go up after launch. For the full rollout, the spending amount is calculated once a day instead of live, so it works for millions of users.

## What I learned

The same information, shown in a different place, gave completely different results. A banner sits on top of what people are doing. A suggestion in the amount buttons is part of it, right where they are already choosing a number.

Next, I want to test showing spending before users open the top-up screen, before they have a number in mind.
